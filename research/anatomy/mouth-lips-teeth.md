# Lips, mouth and dental support

> The mouth is the most expression-sensitive region of the face, so likeness work must fit it at rest first. The anthropometric landmarks are subnasale, labiale superius, the philtral ridges and cheilion [1][4]. The best-supported vertical rule is that the lower vermilion is taller than the upper, by about 1.6:1 in Caucasian-derived norms. That exact ratio is contested across populations [5][6][8], and upper vermilion height shrinks with age [2]. The lips are draped over the dental arch, and incisor position and display at rest affect lip support and how old a face looks [14][15][16]. Classic canons such as "mouth width = 1.5 × nose width" and "intercanthal = nose width" fail for most young adults [3]. No source was found that tests "mouth width = interpupillary distance" at all, so a fitting tool should treat all such canons as weak sanity checks, never as constraints.

## Key facts

- **Philtrum anatomy.** The philtrum is a vertical midline groove of the upper lip with two lateral ridges (philtral columns), running from subnasale to labiale superius (the upper vermilion border). Measure it at rest, because expression moves these landmarks [1]. ⚠️
- **Cupid's bow depends on philtrum depth.** Whether a Cupid's bow shows depends more on philtral depth than on upper-lip fullness, so a deep philtrum can make a bow even on a thin upper lip. This comes from a snippet that was not checked against the full text [1]. ⚠️
- **Upper vermilion is taller at the bow peaks than at the midline.** In a 3D toddler study, upper vermilion height was greater paramedially (at the Cupid's bow peaks) than at the midline. The finding is used for marking in cleft repair. The harvest attributed it to [1], which is a doubtful attribution [1]. ⚠️
- **Adult upper-lip dimensions.** Adults aged 20–59 had philtrum width of about 11–12 mm and upper lip height (subnasale to vermilion) of about 22–23 mm [2]. These are snippet values; the population, n and sex split are unconfirmed. ⚠️
- **Upper vermilion thins with age.** In the same dataset, upper vermilion height fell from about 8.1 mm (ages 20–29) to about 6.6 mm (ages 50–59), a loss of roughly 1.5 mm, or about 19% [2]. ⚠️
- **Direction of the vertical ratio.** The lower vermilion is taller than the upper. Two independent sources agree on the direction [5][6]. ✅ (direction only)
- **Size of the vertical ratio.** The commonly cited lower:upper ratio is about 1.6:1, and some sources give a range of 1.6–2:1. It comes mainly from young Caucasian women [5][6]. A 2024 review argues the ratio does not carry over to other populations and notes that Black individuals tend to have larger lip volume [8]. ↔️ (contested; range 1.6–2.0, population-dependent)
- **The ratio matters perceptually.** In a perception study, an over-filled upper lip looked less artificial when the lower lip was raised to about 1.6× the upper lip height [6]. An augmentation study found that keeping the upper:lower red-lip proportion mattered more than shortening the white (cutaneous) upper lip [7]. ⚠️
- **Mouth-to-nose canon.** Among 1,050 young adults, mouth width exceeded 1.5 × nose width in the majority, with a wide spread. Intercanthal distance was shorter than nose width in most subjects, and the orbital canon variants were spread broadly [3]. In other words, the canons are rules of thumb. ⚠️
- **Population reference values.** A Kenyan normative study measured intercanthal and interpupillary distance as separate variables. It states that current standard reference values do not reflect population diversity [4]. ⚠️
- **Mouth width vs. pupil distance.** The harvest found **no study that directly tests "mouth width = interpupillary distance."** The rule is unvalidated, not confirmed [3][4]. (No badge: this is an absence of evidence.)
- **Lateral upper-lip height.** A Kenyan adult sample reported a lateral upper-lip (commissure) height of 23.5 mm (SD 2.6) in one group and 22.3 mm (SD 2.3) in another. Which group is which (sex or subgroup) was unclear in the snippet [4]. ⚠️
- **Smile arc definition.** The smile arc compares the curve of the maxillary incisal edges and canine tips with the curve of the lower lip in a posed smile. The orthodontic ideal is that the two curves run parallel ("consonant") [11]. ⚠️
- **Smile arc in practice.** How often the arc is consonant varies widely: 63.3% in 150 Saudi orthodontic patients [17][18], 66.9% in Saudi adults of the Jeddah region [19], and 82.8% in one further cohort [17][19][20][21] (the exact source of the 82.8% was not pinned down). Only 43.1% of 490 Vietnamese students aged 18–29 had parallel arcs [20], and 58.3% of finishing-phase orthodontic patients had non-consonant arcs [21]. An older study (Maulik & Nanda, cited in [19]) found straight arcs most common, at 49% [19]. Laypeople in one study rated flat or reverse arcs more favourably, while general dentists preferred flat to mildly curved arcs [12]. ↔️ (contested)
- **Smile arc and skeleton.** A 2026 retrospective study of 150 patients found no significant association between smile-arc type and either sagittal skeletal pattern or maxillary incisor display [17][18]. ⚠️ ([17] and [18] are the same study.)
- **Pose distorts the smile arc.** In a photographic study, anteroposterior head inclination (pitch) changed whether observers judged the smile arc and lower-lip curvature as consonant [22]. ⚠️
- **Incisor display at rest.** A pleasing amount of upper-incisor display with the lips at rest is about 2–4 mm. More display is linked to a gummy smile, and less to an aged look [14]. The esthetic reference in a smile is full display of the upper-incisor crowns with minimal gum showing [13]. ⚠️
- **Incisor angle changes display.** Upright upper incisors tend to show more in a smile, and flared incisors show less [15]. ⚠️
- **Causes of low display.** Low incisor display is associated with poor lip support, reduced vertical dimension, a long upper lip or a retruded maxilla. In one case report, extruding the whole arch improved both display and lip support [16]. ⚠️ (case-level evidence)

## Applying this to photo→3D face sculpting in Blender

1. **Fit the lips only from a neutral, lips-closed photo.** Expression moves the philtrum and lip landmarks [1]. Before measuring, reject or down-weight any photo where the lips are parted, the teeth show, or the corners are raised. Keep smile photos for separate, expression-specific targets (shape keys) only.
2. **Measure first, then compare with norms.** After the solve gives you metric scale (the face3d engine already has a perspective camera with focal estimation), read these values off the fitted mesh:
   - Mouth width (cheilion to cheilion).
   - sn–ls (upper lip height).
   - Upper and lower vermilion heights at the midline and at the Cupid's bow peaks.
   - Philtrum width between the philtral columns.

   Compare them with Tables 1–2 only to flag outliers. Do not pull the mesh toward the norms: the norms are cohort-specific [4][8].
3. **Do not snap the mouth corners to the pupils.** No study shows that mouth width equals pupil distance (see "Mouth width vs. pupil distance" in Key facts), and nose- and eye-based canons fail for most people [3]. Place cheilion from the photo landmarks, and check it across views: in three-quarter views the far corner is foreshortened and often in shadow. (This last point is a practitioner observation; no source was found for it.)
4. **Sculpt the Cupid's bow from the philtrum depth, not from lip volume.** The bow comes from the philtral columns and the groove between them [1]. If the reference shows a sharp bow on a thin lip, deepen the philtrum and raise the column ridges; don't inflate the vermilion. Keep the upper vermilion taller at the bow peaks than at the midline dip [1].
5. **Use age to judge the vermilion.** A subject in their 50s typically has about 1.5 mm less upper vermilion than one in their 20s [2]. A youthful default lip on an older subject is a common source of "it's not them".
6. **Build an arch proxy before sculpting lip projection.** The lips drape over the incisors and canines. Retruded or under-displaying incisors go with poor lip support and an aged look [14][16], and flared incisors change display [15]. If the subject shows 2–4 mm of upper incisor at rest [14] and the model shows none, the upper lip is probably too long or too low.
7. **Smile shape keys.** Do not force the incisal curve parallel to the lower lip. Fewer than half to about four fifths of real smiles are consonant ([17]–[21]), and people do not all prefer the consonant form [12]. Copy the subject's actual arc from smile photos. Check the head pitch first, because pitch changes how the arc reads [22].
8. **Common failures:**
   - Lips fitted from slightly parted or smiling photos.
   - An idealised Caucasian lip ratio imposed on someone else [8].
   - Mouth width taken from the eyes.
   - A smile arc misread because the head was pitched [22].
   - Lips sculpted without teeth underneath, which looks wrong once the jaw opens.

## Algorithmic implications

- **Landmark schema.** Include subnasale, labiale superius, the left and right philtral column tops at the vermilion border, the midline tubercle, stomion, labiale inferius, and the left and right cheilion [1][4]. Store the upper vermilion border as a polyline (peak–dip–peak), not as a single point, so the Cupid's bow shape and the paramedian-vs-midline height difference can be represented [1].
- **Neutral gate.** Compute a lip-apart score and a corner-elevation score from the 2D landmarks. Fit identity shape only on frames that pass the gate; route the others to expression coefficients [1].
- **Soft priors, not constraints.**
  - Lower:upper vermilion ratio: a weak prior centred near 1.6, with a population-conditioned variance wide enough to cover at least 1.6–2.0 and also values below that [5][6][8].
  - Age-conditioned mean for upper vermilion height (Table 1) [2].
  - Do not include any mouth-width = IPD term [3]. If canon checks are kept at all, they should only produce QA warnings.
- **Population awareness.** Expose a reference-population selector, or better, learn priors from multi-ethnic scan data instead of from Farkas-style single-cohort norms [4][8].
- **Dental support as a latent variable.** Parameterise an arch/incisor proxy with these values:
  - Anteroposterior position.
  - Incisor proclination.
  - Vertical position, which sets display at rest. Default target 2–4 mm [14].

  Make lip projection and upper-lip drape depend on this proxy, so that fitting a lip profile can be explained by tooth support and not only by soft-tissue volume [15][16].
- **Pose before curvature.** Estimate head pitch first, then measure the smile arc and lower-lip curvature in a head-aligned frame [22]. Report the arc class (consonant, flat or reverse) as a descriptor, not as a target [11][12].
- **Uncertainty.** Carry the source status per prior (verified, single-source or contested) into the solver as a confidence weight. Every numeric prior in this note is currently single-source or contested.

## Disagreements, myths & refuted claims

- **"Mouth width = interpupillary distance."** Unvalidated: no study testing it was found. Treat it as an artist's rule of thumb [3][4].
- **"Mouth width = 1.5 × nose width" and "intercanthal = nose width."** Both fail for most of 1,050 young adults [3].
- **Lip ratio 1:1.6 as a universal ideal.** Contested. It comes from young Caucasian women, other sources give 1:1.6–1:2, and a 2024 review rejects it as universal [5][6][8]. Wording problem: one harvested snippet gave the StatPearls figure as "upper-to-lower 1.6:1 to 2:1". That contradicts [6], which has the lower lip taller, so this note assumes lower > upper.
- **Smile-arc prevalence.** The older study found straight arcs most common (49%), while later studies report consonant majorities. Differences in how the arcs were classified probably explain this [19].
- **Is a consonant arc the ideal?** Orthodontists treat it as the ideal [11], but laypeople favoured flat or reverse arcs [12].
- **Does the arc depend on display or skeleton?** The 2026 study found no link [17], while the clinical literature ties low display to lip support and skeletal factors [16].
- **Dropped claims:**
  - "Lip-line cant 1.6° (SD 1.0) in Asian adults": no source URL was found for it, so it was excluded.
  - A harvested statement that attributed Saudi facial-thirds canon findings to the 1,050-adult study: the attribution looked wrong, so it was not used.
