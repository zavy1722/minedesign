# Facial proportions, neoclassical canons and the golden-ratio myth

> The neoclassical canons (equal thirds, "five eyes" across, intercanthal = eye = nose width, mouth = 1.5 × nose, ear = nose length) are Renaissance drawing conventions. Every population tested since Farkas's 1985 revision fails most of them most of the time, and even the best one, the orbital canon, holds in only about a third of people [1][4][5][6]. The golden ratio has no evidential support as a facial ideal, and Marquardt's phi mask fits one type of fashion model, not people [11][14][15]. Real faces scatter around population means by roughly 3–12% per measure, and likeness lives in those deviations, so a canon used as a target yields a generic, "beautified" stranger. Use proportions only as weak log-ratio priors for what photos cannot see, as QA flags in a metric, pose-normalised 3D frame, and as block-out scaffolding.
>
> Evidence status: 7 ✅ / 31 📄 / 46 🧠 / 11 ↔️ — 🧠 items are queued for web verification.

## Key facts

Legend: ✅ ≥2 independent input sources agree · 📄 one input source · 🧠 author knowledge, unchecked · ↔️ contested. Harvested facts come from abstracts and snippets, not full texts. Absolute millimetre norms are in `anatomy/anthropometric-norms.md`; this note covers ratios.

**What the canons are**
- Vitruvius divided the face into thirds at the **eyebrows** and the **nose base**; Leonardo and Dürer elaborated the scheme. Farkas et al. (1985) turned it into eight or nine testable caliper equalities (Table A) and first tested them in young North American Caucasian adults [1] 📄. Most held only in a minority of subjects, some in almost nobody; per-canon percentages still need checking 🧠.
- **Definitions decide the verdict.** Farkas's thirds are tr–n, n–sn, sn–gn. Powell & Humphreys' surgical "aesthetic thirds" are tr–g, g–sn, sn–me. Artists use hairline, brow line, nose base and chin. Moving the top of the middle third from nasion up to glabella adds about 10–15 mm and turns a middle:lower ratio of about 1:1.25 into about 1:1.05 🧠. Farkas's soft-tissue gnathion (gn) is the lowest midline chin point, which cephalometrists call soft-tissue menton 🧠.
- **The hairline (trichion) is the weakest landmark.** Recession and styling move it by centimetres, and forehead height is the most ethnically variable neoclassical measure (`anatomy/forehead-brow-temple-hairline.md`). Every canon that uses tr is unreliable 🧠.

**How often they hold** (studies in Table B)
- **Most adults fail most canons in every population tested:** Saudi [1][2], African American women [4], 1,050 Turkish adults [5], and Greek and Chinese, Vietnamese and Thai samples, as reported in [1] ✅.
- **The orbital canon (en–en = ex–en) is met most often, still by a minority.** It held in 30.6% of African American women, the best of nine canons [4], and was the most validated canon in Saudi Class III patients [6] ✅. Al-Sebaei could not validate it in Saudi students [1] ↔️.
- **The thirds are unequal** in Saudi adults (p < 0.0001) [1] 📄, yet one study reports uniform thirds and fifths in attractive women [10] (snippet only) ↔️.
- **The failures are directional** (Table A): in European-descent adults the nasion-based lower third runs 15–30% longer than the middle, and the ear is 5–12 mm longer than the nose 🧠.
- **Population differences are systematic.** Canons built on White reference values ignore age, sex and ethnic variation [8], and Farkas, Forrest & Litsas (2000) said canons must not be strict surgical directives [4] ✅.
- **Norms age, and methods differ.** Weinberg et al. compared Farkas's caliper norms (N = 2,326) with 3D Facial Norms data (N = 2,454) [28] 📄; caliper and 3D-surface values are related, not interchangeable 🧠. Farkas reportedly advised renewing norms every ~20 years (secondary, unverified) ↔️.

**The golden ratio (φ ≈ 1.618)**
- **No evidence supports it.** A 2024 review found none for φ in facial aesthetic or reconstructive planning [14]; in African-Caribbean models only one ratio resembled φ, and none predicted attractiveness [15]; Holland (2008) rejects the phi mask [11] ✅.
- **The phi mask.** Marquardt built it from actors and models and claimed universality [12]. Holland showed the fit method is flawed and the mask fits Europeans poorly, East Asians and sub-Saharan Africans worse, and best matches masculinised White female fashion models; he proposed generalised Procrustes analysis with thin-plate splines [11] 📄.
- New "golden proportions" keep being published [13] 📄; one 2022 paper argues φ may be an ecological affordance [17] ↔️.
- **Why φ always seems to appear.** Twenty landmarks give 190 distances and about 18,000 distance ratios. If log-ratios spread over 1–4, about 4–6% land within ±3% of φ by chance: hundreds per face 🧠 (arithmetic).
- **Face length ÷ width** (hairline–chin ÷ bizygomatic) averages about 1.3–1.4, not 1.62. A "golden" face would need to be about 15–20% narrower than average, although some other width (temporal, say) will hit 1.62, which is measurement shopping again 🧠.
- Pallett, Link & Lee (2010) found the preferred eye–mouth distance ≈ 36% of face length and interpupillary distance ≈ 46% of face width: population averages, not φ [30] 📄 (46% width ratio in the abstract; the 36% length figure is stated in a secondary source, [31]; Caucasian female faces only).
- A Malaysian study (Alam et al. 2015, PLoS One; 286 Malaysian Chinese, Malay and Indian adults aged 18–25) found only 17.1% of facial proportions conformed to φ, with no association between φ conformity and self-rated facial appearance [18] 📄 (attribution confirmed in verification).

**Averageness, beauty and identity**
- **Averageness predicts attractiveness**, reportedly even with symmetry held constant [25][26]. One paper calls it necessary and sufficient [25], but Perrett, May & Yoshikawa (1994) found that exaggerating the shape of attractive composites beat the average ↔️.
- **Likeness is deviation from the average.** Caricatures, which exaggerate how a face differs from the norm, are recognised as well as or better than accurate drawings (Brennan 1985; Rhodes, Brennan & Carey 1987). "Correcting" a sculpt toward a canon is anti-caricature 🧠 (`anatomy/face-perception-likeness.md`).
- Facial symmetry did not correlate with recorded health in a 2001 study [27] 📄. Symmetry is no rule either (`anatomy/facial-asymmetry.md`).

**Artist head construction**
- **Proko** puts the eye line at half the head height, notes that a textbook puts it through the lower lids instead, and warns that perspective compresses thirds and halves [20] 📄; he teaches the head as simplified volumes [22] 📄. **Loomis** (secondary summaries) builds from a cranial ball with a brow-line/centreline cross and stresses that individuals deviate from the average head [23] 📄. Rules and measured reality are compared in Table C.
- **Measured reality:** the adult eye line sits 0.48–0.53 of vertex–chin height down from the top, much lower in children (`anatomy/child-growth.md`) 🧠 ↔️. "Five eyes" matches head breadth, not bizygomatic face width 🧠.

Pitch matters as much as distance. In orthographic projection, 10° of pitch changes the projected middle:lower-third ratio by about 5–10%, because the two segments lean in opposite directions 🧠. Both errors exceed any canon's tolerance.

## Applying this to photo→3D face sculpting in Blender

1. **Solve first, measure second.** One photo's proportions are contaminated by lens and pose (Table D); measure only on the multi-view-solved mesh 🧠.
2. **Normalise the frame.** Level to Frankfort horizontal (`anatomy/craniometric-landmarks.md`) and set metric scale (iris ≈ 11.7 ± 0.5 mm, or a ruler in shot). Read heights on the head's vertical axis, widths on its lateral axis, Farkas comparisons as 3D chords 🧠.
3. **Proportion sheet.** Empties at en, ex, al, ch, sn, sto, n, g, gn, zy, go, sa, sba (tr and v at low trust); an add-on panel lists each Table E ratio with its z-score. At |z| > 2.5 re-check the photos and camera solve, not the sculpt; at |z| > 3.5 suspect a landmark or lens error. Never auto-correct toward the norm 🧠.
4. **Protect the deviations.** The 3–5 largest-|z| ratios are this person's caricature directions: lock them and warn on drift > 2% or 1 mm while sculpting 🧠.
5. **Canons only where photos are blind:** skull top and back under hair, a hidden ear, a chin under a beard. Use wide sigmas and label them as guesses in the UI 🧠.
6. **Overlay measured lines, not canon lines:** guides at the subject's solved brow, en, sn, sto and gn heights in camera-matched orthographic views; spot-check chords with the Measure tool 🧠.
7. **Shoot neutral.** A smile widens ch–ch by roughly 20–30%; age lengthens nose and ears, and tooth loss can shorten the lower third 🧠.

**What goes wrong**
- Sculptors unconsciously regularise: equal thirds (a shortened lower face), en–en = ex–en, a narrowed nose, raised eyes, mirrored halves. The result is a prettier, average stranger 🧠.
- Ratios are read from wide-angle or pitched photos, or before FH levelling; a 10° nod moves the ears about 17 mm relative to the brow (`anatomy/ear.md`).
- Definitions get mixed: nasion with glabella, gnathion with menton, chords with projections 🧠.
- One population's norms are applied to another [8][4] ✅.

## Algorithmic implications

- **Evidence hierarchy:** calibrated multi-view data > statistical shape model (SSM) > conditioned ratio priors > artist canons (block-out only) > golden ratio (never) 🧠.
- **Log-space ratio priors, robust loss:** `E = Σ_k ρ_t((log(d_a/d_b) − log μ_k) / σ_k)`, Student-t with ν ≈ 4 so an unusual face is not pulled in. With honest sigmas no per-term weights are needed 🧠.
- **Shrinkage check.** A prior keeps only k = σp²/(σp² + σd²) of a true deviation (`anatomy/face-perception-likeness.md`). At σp = 0.08 and a two-view ratio error σd ≈ 0.03, k ≈ 0.88: a 2σ-unusual en–en loses about 0.7 mm. Once a ratio is seen in ≥ 2 calibrated views, loosen its prior ×2 or drop it; a leave-prior-out refit should move no observed measure by more than 0.3 mm 🧠.
- **No double counting.** With an SSM prior (BFM, FLAME), canon ratios are correlated projections of the same information: diagnostics only, or σ ×2 🧠.
- **Frames and units.** Vertical ratios in the FH frame, widths as 3D chords; ratios until scale is solved, millimetres only after 🧠.
- **Conditioning.** Separate μ by sex and age band (`anatomy/child-growth.md`). The user picks the norm set; the default is pooled with the "any population" σ. Never infer ancestry from the face 🧠.
- **Template warping.** Start from the SSM or sex-specific mean head scaled by ex–ex and n–gn, then warp to solved landmarks with an RBF or thin-plate spline (Holland's machinery [11]). Canon-perfect templates bias the fit 🧠.
- **No φ terms**, ever, in the likeness pipeline 🧠.

## Disagreements, myths & open questions

- **Attractive faces and canons:** measured populations fail them [1][4][5]; attractive women reportedly show uniform thirds and fifths [10]; an ideal Instagram face matched the "gold standard" [16] ↔️. The 2019 Miss Universe study [9] was not read.
- **Orbital canon:** most validated [4][6] vs not validated [1] ↔️. **Eye line:** head centre vs lower lids [20]; adults sit at or just below the midpoint ↔️.
- **Golden ratio:** rejected [11][14][15], defended as an affordance [17], persisting in the Ricketts–Marquardt tradition [12][13] ↔️.
- **Myths:** a φ-shaped face, a universal phi mask, equal thirds, five eyes across the face, eyes exactly at half.
- **Open:** Farkas 1985 per-canon percentages; the 20-year caveat; whether 3D average faces preserve mean linear measures [24]; anthropometric vs cephalometric definitions of ideals [29]; multi-ethnic 3D ratio norms (3DFN is European-ancestry only).
