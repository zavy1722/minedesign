# Facial aging mechanics

> The face ages in layers, and each layer changes on its own schedule. Bone recedes at the orbital rims, the maxilla, the pyriform aperture and, after tooth loss, the mandible. Fat compartments deflate or grow unevenly. Retaining ligaments hold their lines while the tissue between them loosens. Skin thins, loses elasticity and keeps the creases of repeated expressions. Likeness depends on getting this order right: an older face is a different volume and contour first and a wrinkle texture second, so age is best modelled as a few anatomical parameters (bone, deflation, laxity, skin) rather than one "old" shape. For a consenting subject, chronological age, dental status and cosmetic history should be asked; photo-based age estimation is a fallback prior.

## Key facts

**Skeleton**
- Orbit: the aperture widens and its area grows with age. Resorption concentrates in the superomedial and inferolateral rim, so the medial upper lid hollows and the lateral lower lid loses support 🧠 (Kahn and Shaw 2008).
- Maxilla: cross-sectional CT shows the glabellar, pyriform and maxillary angles decreasing (the midface retrudes and rotates clockwise relative to the cranial base). The cheek and the top of the nasolabial fold lose their bony platform 🧠 (Shaw and Kahn 2007; Pessa 2000). Magnitudes are reported in single-digit degrees between young and old cohorts ↔️ (verify).
- Pyriform aperture: it enlarges, mostly inferolaterally. The alar base sets back, the columella–lip angle closes and the tip droops 🧠.
- Mandible: ramus height, body height and body length fall and the gonial angle opens a few degrees; most change follows tooth loss ↔️ (Shaw et al. 2010; see jaw-chin-neck note). After extraction, the mandibular residual ridge loses height several times faster than the maxillary one 🧠 (Tallgren 1972). Edentulous faces shorten, lips invert, and the chin swings forward and up 🧠.
- Not all adult bone shrinks: longitudinal cephalometry found slight continued craniofacial enlargement in adults ↔️ (Behrents 1985).
- Resting upper-incisor display falls with age (≈3 mm in the 20s to ≈0–1 mm by 60) while lower-incisor display rises 🧠 (Vig and Brundo 1978). Too little upper display reads as aged [2] 📄. Incisor display has been studied directly as a perceived-age cue [22] 📄.

**Fat**
- The superficial and deep fat compartments age separately. Deep medial cheek fat and SOOF lose volume, which removes support under the tear trough and nasolabial fold 🧠 (Rohrich, Pessa and Ristow 2008). CT shows superficial midface compartments sitting lower in older people ↔️ (Gierloff 2012; some of this may be deflation that only looks like descent).
- Loss is uneven. Periorbital, temporal, forehead, lateral cheek and perioral fat atrophy; jowl, submental and sometimes nasolabial fat grow 🧠 (Donofrio 2000). The youthful "inverted triangle" drifts toward a square or pear 🧠.
- Orbital fat bulges through a lax septum. One MRI series also found orbital fat volume increasing with age ↔️ (Darcy 2008).
- BMI changes the picture: lean faces skeletonise (orbit rims, zygomatic arch and temporal crest show) while heavier faces hide bone loss and show more jowl and submental mass 🧠. Higher BMI after about 40 made identical twins look younger 🧠 (Guyuron 2009).

**Muscles and ligaments**
- Depressor pull (DAO, platysma, buccinator) moves the modiolus down and inward and deepens the labiomandibular (marionette) fold [4][5][6] ✅.
- Whether ageing muscles weaken (atrophy) or shorten with higher resting tone is debated; the 2025 review argues for chronic shortening and links muscle weakening to bone loss [6][7] ↔️. Resting masseter thickness is lower after 55 than at 18–35 [8] 📄, and MRI temporalis thickness spans 3.7–16.9 mm across ages 35–84, tracking weight and BMI [9] 📄; muscle loss adds to temporal hollowing and a softer jaw angle 🧠.
- Retaining ligaments act as fixed lines; the masses between them deflate and slide. The lateral face (parotid, masseter, temporal fascia) barely moves, and change concentrates in front of the masseteric ligament line 🧠 (Mendelson; smas-ligaments note).
- Lateral brow ptosis is the classic teaching 🧠 (Knize 1996), but photographic studies found the brow, especially in women, stays level or rises with age through frontalis compensation ↔️ (Matros 2009). Forehead wrinkle severity tracks upper-lid ptosis [19] 📄, which fits that compensation.
- The lower lid lengthens vertically and the lid–cheek junction becomes a visible "double convexity" (lid bag above the groove, malar mass below). Serial photographs suggest the junction itself stays close to the bone while volume changes above and below it ↔️ (Lambros 2007).
- Canthal laxity rounds the lateral canthus (scleral show); levator stretch raises the lid crease and deepens the sulcus 🧠.

**Soft-tissue thickness**
- Whether total thickness changes with age depends on the study. Greek CT [11] and Thai ultrasound over 50 [12] found little age effect; Chinese CT (1174 adults) [13] and Azerbaijani CT (more than 2 mm thicker at zygion and gonion in older adults) [10] found significant effects ↔️. BMI is the strongest single predictor [17] 📄. Net depth changes a few mm at most; redistribution matters more 🧠.
- Facial skin is ≈1.1–1.9 mm thick (mid-cheek dermis 1.88 mm in men, 1.58 mm in women) [15][16] ✅. Dermal collagen falls by roughly 1% a year in adults 🧠 (Shuster 1975), with a faster drop in the first years after menopause 🧠 (Brincat).

**Skin and wrinkles**
- Dynamic lines run perpendicular to the muscle fibres under them: horizontal forehead lines (frontalis), vertical glabellar "11s" (corrugator) and a horizontal nasal-root line (procerus), radial crow's feet (orbicularis oculi), vertical lip lines (orbicularis oris), bunny lines (nasalis) 🧠. Repeated frowning and smiling raise corrugator activity and glabellar wrinkling [20] 📄. Frontalis displacement varies (in 53% of one Asian cohort only the lower forehead moved), so forehead-line patterns differ by person [18] 📄.
- Glogau photoaging types: I no wrinkles (20s–30s), II wrinkles in motion (late 30s–40s), III wrinkles at rest (50s+), IV only wrinkles (60s–70s+) 🧠 (Glogau 1996).
- Sleep lines run obliquely across the forehead, cheek or chest, ignore muscle direction and are often one-sided 🧠. Horizontal neck "necklace" lines can appear in the 20s 🧠.
- Photoaging (sun, smoking) adds coarse wrinkles, elastosis, lentigines and uneven colour. Darker skin types tend to show wrinkles a decade or more later and age more through volume and pigment change 🧠 ↔️.
- Upper vermilion height falls from ≈8.1 mm (20–29) to ≈6.6 mm (50–59) [1] 📄; the skin lip lengthens, the bow flattens and the corners turn down 🧠.
- Ears lengthen ≈0.2 mm a year 🧠 (Heathcote 1995), against an ear-biometrics claim that ear size and shape hold steady until about 70 [21] ↔️. Lobe ptosis adds to the length 🧠.
- Facial asymmetry rises with age (mean RMSD 0.8 ± 0.2 mm in 191 people aged 0.3–88) [3] 📄.

**Perceived age**
- People judge age from shape, skin texture, colour evenness and eye/lip luminance contrast, which falls with age 🧠 (Porcheron 2013; Fink 2006). Human estimates in unconstrained photos are typically off by about 5–8 years; good algorithms reach about 2–3 years on curated benchmarks and 4–6 years in the wild ↔️ (time-sensitive).

## Applying this to photo→3D face sculpting in Blender

1. **Ask first.** Record age, dental status (own teeth, implants, dentures worn in photos?), botulinum toxin (suppresses dynamic lines for months, can lift the lateral brow), fillers, threads, lifts, recent weight change, sun and smoking. Without these the anatomy above gets misread 🧠.
2. **Capture.** Upright, Frankfort horizontal, relaxed neutral with lips lightly closed, plus lips apart at rest for incisor display. Add AU1+2, AU4, AU6+12, AU9 and a pucker to map dynamic lines. Use soft frontal light plus one raking side light for texture. Reject beauty-filtered or retouched frames (no pore-scale detail) 🧠.
3. **Measure per view.** Lower-lid margin to lid–cheek junction; brow height above the pupil; upper vermilion height; incisor display; jowl deviation from the mandibular border line in 3/4; cervicomental angle in profile; ear length; temporal concavity on the silhouette. Each is an age readout, so compare it with Table 2 🧠.
4. **Sculpt in anatomical order.** Bone (orbit rims, maxilla, mandible and alveolus) → deep fat (SOOF, deep medial cheek, buccal, temporal) → superficial compartments → laxity (Grab or Elastic Deform with the ligament strips masked, moving tissue inferomedially in the midface and inferiorly at the jowl) → skin folds → wrinkles on Multires. Grooves should appear where a held line meets a moved mass 🧠.
5. **Wrinkle craft.** Lay lines perpendicular to the muscle and copy the person's own pattern from the expression frames. Give each crease a sharp bottom, rounded shoulders and a fuller overhanging side (cheek over the NLF, lid skin over the crease). Vary length, depth and spacing; break lines; keep left–right differences. Cavity auto-masking keeps crepe passes in recesses (Blender 3.5+; check current version) 🧠.
6. **Make age a slider.** Store bone, deflation, laxity and skin as separate shape keys or Multires layers driven by one `age` control, so the likeness can be aged or de-aged. Detail on Multires follows the base when the base shape keys change 🧠 (check with your Blender version).
7. **Check under the photo's light.** Match camera and key light before judging hollows: top light invents tear troughs and jowls 🧠.

**What goes wrong:** ageing by wrinkles alone (a young volume under old skin looks uncanny); sagging everything straight down, which loses the fixed lateral face; carving folds as V-cuts; symmetric or evenly spaced lines; deep fine lines in the mesh; jowls from a chin-down photo; edentulous collapse on someone who wears dentures; forgetting the longer nose, ears and upper lip; lean skeletal ageing on a heavy face, or the reverse; dynamic lines baked into the neutral 🧠.

## Algorithmic implications

- **Age input.** `age_years` comes from the subject (σ ≈ 1 y). Otherwise use an ensemble apparent-age estimate, σ ≈ 6 y, widened for makeup, filters, harsh light or under-represented skin types 🧠. Sculpt apparent age; use chronological age only for priors on what photos cannot see, such as the bony orbit.
- **Age basis, separate from identity** (names match `smas-ligaments.md`), with all 🧠 placeholder priors to calibrate on scans:
  - `laxity` ∈ [0,1], prior N(clip((age−25)/50, 0, 1), 0.2).
  - `deep_cheek_volume`: ±3 mm along the normal, mean −0.04·max(0, age−35) mm, σ 1.5 mm.
  - `orbital_fat_prolapse` ∈ [0,1].
  - `temporal_hollow`: 0–4 mm, raised for low BMI.
  - `orbit_rim_recession`: inferolateral and superomedial 0–3 mm, mean 0 below 40, then rising, σ 1 mm.
  - `maxilla_retrusion_deg`: 0–10, σ 3 ↔️.
  - `mandible_loss`: ramus and body height, gonial opening 0–5°, σ 2°; gated by `dental_status`.
  - `alveolar_collapse_mm`: 0 for dentate subjects; several mm to >10 mm in edentulous subjects without dentures ↔️.
  - `skin_age` ∈ [0,1]: texture and displacement only, never in the mesh fit.
- **Landmark priors that move with age:** ls–sto μ = 8.0 − 0.05·(age−25) mm, σ 1.5 [1]; upper-incisor display μ = max(0, 3 − 0.07·(age−25)) mm, σ 1.2; ear length + 0.2·(age−25) mm, σ 3 ↔️; lid–cheek junction distance growing with age at σ 3 mm ↔️; lateral brow height N(young value, 4 mm), with no forced descent ↔️.
- **Thickness model:** keep the age term on total soft-tissue depth small and site-specific, N(0, 1.5 mm), larger at zygion and gonion [10]; put age into redistribution (the basis above), not net loss ↔️.
- **Symmetry:** loosen the symmetry prior with age, e.g. σ_sym = σ₀·(1 + 0.01·(age−25)) [3] (scaling heuristic 🧠).
- **Wrinkles in fitting:** fit the mesh on contours and landmarks; down-weight photometric terms over fold and wrinkle regions; detect fine lines with a ridge filter only in raking-light frames. Split detail into static (present in neutral) and expression-dependent (DECA-style) layers and store dynamic lines as per-AU corrective maps 🧠.
- **Statistical age directions:** BFM 2009 ships linear attribute directions (age, weight, gender); FLAME has no age parameter, so an age direction must be regressed from aged scans. Check data licences 🧠 (time-sensitive).
- **Edit order and reversibility:** apply bone → deep fat → superficial fat → laxity → skin; de-age in reverse. Clamp `laxity` so tissue never crosses ligament strips 🧠.

## Disagreements, myths & open questions

- **Gravity vs deflation vs bone.** Ligament laxity (Furnas, Mendelson), fat deflation (Lambros, Rohrich and Pessa), skeletal retrusion (Shaw and Kahn, Pessa) and muscle tone [6] all have evidence ↔️; model each with its own parameter.
- **Is bone loss real?** Skeletal studies are mostly cross-sectional, so cohort differences (dentition, diet, ancestry) can masquerade as ageing; Behrents' longitudinal data show slight enlargement ↔️. Dentate mandible change is unresolved.
- **Soft-tissue thickness:** limited [11][12] vs significant [10][13] age effects ↔️.
- **Brow:** classic ptosis vs measured stability or elevation ↔️ (Matros 2009).
- **Orbital fat:** prolapse only vs real volume gain ↔️ (Darcy 2008).
- **Ears:** continuous growth vs stable to 70 [21] ↔️.
- **Myth: wrinkles make age.** Contour, lip and eye proportions and colour contrast carry age too; a smooth older face still reads old.
- **Myth: everything sags straight down.** The midface moves inferomedially, the lateral face is held, and the jowl sits between the masseteric and mandibular ligaments.
- **Open:** a longitudinal 3D dataset of the same people across decades with a usable licence (none known); bone-ageing magnitudes outside European cohorts; compartment volume curves by age and BMI; whether the muscle-tone model [6] holds quantitatively.
