# Classic art-anatomy references for heads

> The classic head references fall into three families. Construction canons (Loomis, the Reilly method, Bridgman) give a fast, idealized scaffold. Plane models (the Asaro head, Reilly's rhythm lines) teach how form turns into light. Anatomical atlases (Peck, Goldfinger, Scott Spencer, Uldis Zarins) explain the skull, muscle and fat behind those forms, and Zarins and Faigin add expression. The numeric canons describe one idealized adult head with no variance, and anthropometric studies show that most real faces fail most canons [5][7][8]. For photo→3D likeness, use them in three ways only: as a block-in initializer, as the vocabulary and region map for checks and UI, and as weak priors where the photos cannot see. Never use them as targets. The usual professional advice: Loomis (often via Proko) for construction, the Asaro head for planes, Goldfinger, Peck or Zarins for anatomy, Spencer for the digital-sculpting workflow, Faigin or Zarins for expression, and Bridgman for mass and gesture once the basics are known.

## Key facts

**Loomis (construction canon)**
- The cranium is a ball with a centerline. Where the centerline crosses the brow line is the key construction point for the whole head [4] 📄. The sides of the ball are sliced off as flat discs (the side planes), and the face hangs below the ball 🧠
- The eye line sits at half the total head height. Equivalently, it sits one quarter of the way down the middle third [1] 📄. A textbook that Proko cites puts the eye line through the bottoms of the lids instead, and Proko notes the conflict [1] ↔️
- The mouth line falls in the top third of the lower third (subnasale→chin divided into 3) [2] 📄
- The head is about five eye-widths across at eye level, with one eye-width between the eyes. The ears sit in the middle third and usually end at the outside of the original circle [4] 📄
- Thirds and halves compress in perspective, so construction ratios change with viewing angle [1] 📄. Practitioner sources stress that individuals depart from the average head [4] 📄
- In the "ideal" adult male, the head is 3½ units tall: crown→hairline ½, hairline→brow 1, brow→nose base 1, nose base→chin 1. This puts the eye line exactly at half height and is consistent with [1] 🧠
- Books: *Fun with a Pencil* (1939), *Figure Drawing for All It's Worth* (1943), *Drawing the Head and Hands* (1956, the head reference). Titan Books has reissued them in facsimile since the 2010s 🧠. Proko's video courses are the main modern channel for Loomis, Asaro and Reilly material [1][2]. They also teach a ten-volume breakdown of the head [3] 📄 (known from the lesson title only)

**Reilly method**
- Frank J. Reilly (1906–1967) taught at the Art Students League of New York and studied there under Bridgman, among others. He published no book, so the method survives through his students: Jack Faragasso's books and teachers' class notes. Versions differ from teacher to teacher 🧠 ↔️
- The "Reilly abstraction" lays a small set of named, flowing rhythm lines over a ball-and-plane head: the brow arc, the eye-socket ovals, a cheek line running from the outer brow down around the muzzle toward the chin, the mouth barrel and the jaw line. The lines encode plane boundaries and rhythm, not measurements 🧠

**Asaro head**
- The Asaro head is a planar head sculpture by John Asaro, a Reilly student. It is sold as a cast teaching tool in several variants (male, female, and others; check the current line-up). It reduces the head to flat facets so that every value change under one light maps to a plane change 🧠 ↔️
- The plane layout is an artistic simplification, not an anatomical partition, and other sculptors' planar heads differ 🧠. It is a copyrighted commercial sculpture, so do not ship a facet-for-facet copy 🧠 ↔️

**Bridgman**
- George B. Bridgman (1865–1943) taught at the Art Students League for about four decades. His head books: *Constructive Anatomy* (1920), *Life Drawing* (1924), *Heads, Features and Faces* (1932), *The Human Machine* (1939). All are in Dover reprints 🧠
- His method treats masses as blocks that wedge and interlock: the cranial box with front, side and top planes, the face wedge, and the neck driving into the skull. It is strong on movement and mass and loose on anatomical accuracy, so it is usually recommended after a precise atlas 🧠

**Peck**
- Stephen Rogers Peck, *Atlas of Human Anatomy for the Artist* (Oxford University Press, 1951; still in print). It is a compact atlas with clear plates of the skull and facial muscles, proportion charts and a section on facial expression. It is widely recommended as a first accurate atlas 🧠

**Scott Spencer**
- Spencer is a Gnomon instructor and film creature sculptor. His books are *ZBrush Character Creation* (Sybex, 2008; 2nd ed. 2011) and *ZBrush Digital Sculpting Human Anatomy* (Sybex/Wiley, 2010) 🧠
- He teaches "inside-out" head sculpting: skull landmarks first, then facial muscles, then fat pads and skin. The skull is shown as the driver of surface landmarks (brow ridge, zygomatic arch, mandible angle). This is the reference closest to a Blender sculpt workflow 🧠

**Uldis Zarins**
- Uldis Zarins (with Sandis Kondrats) wrote *Anatomy for Sculptors: Understanding the Human Figure* (2014, Exonicus/Anatomy4Sculptors) and later *Anatomy of Facial Expression* (≈2017, Anatomy Next/Exonicus). Check the year, co-authorship and publisher 🧠 ↔️
- *Anatomy of Facial Expression* works muscle by muscle: each muscle's origin and insertion, its pull, and the skin deformation and wrinkle pattern it makes, built up into compound emotions. The images are 3D-render style. Wrinkles run roughly perpendicular to the pull of the muscle fibres 🧠
- *Anatomy for Sculptors* reduces muscles and fat to sculptable simplified forms. It is the book 3D artists cite most for "form, not just names" 🧠

**Also widely recommended for heads**
- Eliot Goldfinger, *Human Anatomy for Artists* (OUP, 1991): often called the most accurate art atlas, with surface-form detail and fat compartments. Gary Faigin, *The Artist's Complete Guide to Facial Expression* (1990): the classic expression reference. Valerie Winslow, *Classic Human Anatomy* (2009). For likeness in clay, Lanteri's modelling volumes (caliper measurement from the sitter) and Peter Rubino's *The Portrait in Clay* (1997) 🧠

**What anthropometry says about the canons**
- The neoclassical canons fail in most subjects measured in several populations: Saudi [5], African American women [7] and Turkish adults (n = 1,050) [8] ✅
- Of the canons tested, the orbital canon (en–en = en–ex) performs best. Even so, only 30.6% of African American women met it [7], and it was the most validated canon in Saudi Class III patients [9] ✅
- In 168 Saudi adults the thirds were unequal (p < 0.0001) [5] 📄. Forehead height shows the largest variation between ethnic groups [10] 📄
- Farkas's norms come from 2,326 North American White subjects (direct anthropometry), so canon "averages" carry that population [18] 📄. Intercanthal reference standards built on White values do not capture variation by age, sex or ethnicity [17] 📄
- There is no evidence for the golden ratio in facial planning [15]. Marquardt's phi mask fits non-European faces poorly [16] ✅

## Applying this to photo→3D face sculpting in Blender

1. **Solve the camera before judging proportions.** A canon checked on a raw photo mixes up pitch, lens and identity [1]. Arm's-length selfies inflate the nose and shrink the ears. Measure ratios only on the fitted 3D mesh, in a Frankfort-horizontal frame 🧠
2. **Block in like Loomis, then let the photos win.** From clicks on front and profile photos (vertex, glabella, nasion, subnasale, menton, tragion, opisthocranion, both zygia), fit the cranial sphere, side planes, face plane and jaw wedge. Then warp the template to the photo landmarks with canon priors near zero 🧠
3. **Use the measurement set** en–ex, en–en, ex–ex, al–al, ch–ch, n–sn, sn–sto, sto–me, n–me, zy–zy, go–go, sa–sba, and the tragion-to-nasion depth. Show each as subject value, canon value and population z-score. A large |z| marks a likeness feature, so preserve it and never "correct" it 🧠
4. **Plane check (Asaro/Reilly logic).** Render with one key light matched to the photo's key, in Workbench with a single studio light or in EEVEE with one area light. Compare where the terminator falls on the cheek front-to-side turn, the brow, the temporal plane and the mouth barrel. Plane errors show up in shading long before landmark error does 🧠
5. **Profile checks that frontal photos cannot constrain:**
   - glabella and nasion depth
   - nasal tip projection
   - lips against the nose-tip–chin line (Ricketts E-line: in many adults the lips sit a few mm behind it, varying by ethnicity and age)
   - chin projection
   - gonial angle
   - ear set-back and tilt
   - depth of the occiput

   🧠 ↔️
6. **Eyes.** The corneal apex sits about 12–20 mm in front of the lateral orbital rim (Hertel normals vary by population). The lateral canthus is usually set back from, and slightly higher than, the medial canthus. Eyes that sit too far forward or too level are among the most common tells 🧠
7. **Frequent failures:**
   - Loomis-ization: averaged and symmetric, with the identity gone
   - the eye line placed by rule
   - mouth corners stretched under the pupils
   - ears placed between brow and nose when the subject's are lower or longer
   - a flat face front with no mouth barrel
   - the hairline used as an anchor
   - expression baked into identity (a smile widens ch–ch and lifts the cheeks)

   🧠

## Algorithmic implications

- **Each canon is a soft prior record.** Store `{name, landmarks_A, landmarks_B, stated_ratio, pop_mean, pop_sd, population, n, source_urls, status, weight}`. Art books supply `stated_ratio` only. `pop_mean` and `pop_sd` come from anthropometry. A loader must refuse a non-zero weight on `status: knowledge` until it is verified 🧠
- **Energy.** `E_canon = Σ_i w_i ((r_i − μ_i)/σ_i)²`, with `w_i` falling as photo coverage of that dimension rises (for example `w_i = w0 · (1 − coverage_i)`, `w0 ≈ 0.05–0.1` relative to the landmark term). In a frontal-only fit, the depth priors (profile square, nasal projection, ear set-back) carry the load. Canons are never hard constraints 🧠
- **Sizing σ.** Linear facial dimensions have a coefficient of variation of about 4–8% [11][12][13]. A ratio of two measures has CV ≈ √(c₁² + c₂²) less a correlation term, which gives σ ≈ 8–12% of μ for horizontal ratios (Table 3). Use 0.02 for the normalized eye line in adults. Widen σ by about 1.5× when sex or population is unknown 🧠
- **Gating.** Disable the vertical canons for children, where the eye line drops below 0.5. Store population tags. Never use trichion as a weighted landmark 🧠
- **Loomis rig as initializer.** Parameters: cranial radius R, side-plane offset (about 0.7–0.8 R), brow height, unit length, jaw width at gonion, chin projection and head pitch. Solve from 6–8 clicks across views, then hand off to template warping or a statistical shape model. A learned PCA model encodes the co-variation that canons ignore, so it should replace canons wherever it is available 🧠
- **Plane regions.** Author your own roughly 20–30 named vertex groups: forehead front and sides, temporal, brow ridge, orbital, upper lid, infraorbital, zygomatic front and side, nasal dorsum, sides, tip and alae, maxillary barrel, upper and lower lip, mental, mandibular side and ramus. Use them for per-region regularization, a per-region shading-residual check under a single light, and named sculpt masks. Do not copy Asaro facets 🧠
- **Expression layer.** Mirror the Zarins/Faigin muscle→effect tables as muscle → FACS AU → shape key. Hold all AUs at 0 when fitting identity. Mark residuals that look like expression (corners up, cheeks raised, brows in) and ask for a neutral photo 🧠
- **Deviation report as a feature.** Rank the |z| values and name them in art vocabulary ("wide-set eyes", "long lower third"). This is how the books become an explanation layer rather than a target 🧠

## Disagreements, myths & open questions

- **Eye line.** At half height in adults: Proko and Loomis place eye centres there, while one textbook uses the lid bottoms [1] ↔️. Measured adults sit at half or slightly below 🧠
- **Thirds and fifths.** These are teaching devices. Measured thirds are unequal [5]. The fifths rule depends on whether "head width" means zy–zy or eu–eu ↔️
- **Mouth width.** Corners under the pupils, under the medial limbus, or at 1.5 eye-gaps? The data favour narrower than interpupillary [14] ↔️
- **Golden ratio and phi mask.** Unsupported as a target [15][16] ✅
- **Bridgman.** Inspiring or misleading for beginners? Practitioner opinion is split 🧠 ↔️
- **Do planar models help likeness?** They help with lighting and form. Likeness comes from measurement, which is the Lanteri/Rubino tradition 🧠
- **Open:**
  - the exact Reilly line set
  - the current Asaro variants and their licence
  - the publication details of Zarins's book
  - US renewal status for Loomis
  - measured adult values for the normalized vertical levels in Table 2 from ANSUR II, Farkas or 3DFN
  - whether any study compares the Asaro planes with the normals of mean scanned faces
